TWCEI & Global Rank Trend (2017–2023)
The solid line (left axis) shows the economy's global rank among 62 economies — a falling number means moving up in the ranking (rank 1 = most efficient). The dashed line (right axis) shows the Trade-Weighted Carbon Efficiency Index (TWCEI) in constant 2010 M$/Gg CO₂-eq — a rising value means each dollar of exports embodies less carbon.
SDA Waterfall: Drivers of Carbon Change (2017→2023)
The waterfall decomposes the total change in embodied carbon into three additive effects. Δf emission intensity — did sectors produce more cleanly? ΔB production structure — did supply chain linkages shift? ΔE trade volume — did export scale expand or contract? Green bars reduce carbon; red bars increase it. Note: Δf captures global intensity changes applied to this economy's export base.
Sector-Level Carbon Efficiency Index (2017 vs 2023)
Each bar shows the Carbon Efficiency Index (CEI = output multiplier ÷ carbon multiplier) for one of 35 sectors, in constant 2010 M$/Gg CO₂-eq. A longer bar means more economic output per unit of embodied carbon — higher is greener. Compare the paired bars: a longer 2023 bar means the sector became more efficient over the period. Service sectors (finance, telecom, education) typically show high CEI; energy-intensive sectors (electricity, metals, petroleum) show low CEI.
Structural Decomposition Analysis (SDA) decomposes the total change in global embodied carbon into three additive effects using the polar decomposition of Dietzenbacher & Los (1998): Δf emission intensity of production, ΔB Leontief production structure, and ΔE trade volume and composition. The three effects sum exactly to the total change — there is no residual. All values are in Gg CO₂-eq, constant 2010 USD.
Global Embodied Carbon Decomposition (2017→2023)
The waterfall chart starts at total global embodied carbon in 2017 (C₂₀₁₇) and shows how each factor contributed to reaching the 2023 level (C₂₀₂₃). Δf measures how changes in direct GHG intensity (emissions per unit of output) across all 63 economies affected total carbon. ΔB captures how shifts in the Leontief input-output structure — upstream supply chain reorganization — changed carbon propagation. ΔE measures the effect of changes in export volumes and composition. Green bars = carbon reduction; red bars = carbon increase.
SDA by Sector (2017→2023)
Each row shows how the three SDA effects operated within one of 35 sectors across all economies. Bars extending left = that effect reduced embodied carbon in this sector; bars extending right = that effect increased it. The stacked bars sum to the sector's total carbon change (ΔC). Sectors at the top of the chart had the largest carbon increases; those at the bottom had the largest reductions. Use this chart to identify which sectors and which drivers (intensity vs. structure vs. trade volume) dominate the global decomposition.
Top Trade Corridors
The table shows SDA results for key bilateral trade corridors. C₂₀₁₇ / C₂₀₂₃ = total embodied carbon in that corridor's exports (Gg CO₂-eq). ΔC = net change. The three effect columns show what drove that change. Red values = carbon increase; green values = carbon reduction.

How to read this tab: These charts map the physical carbon burden of global trade — not which goods are traded, but how much CO₂-equivalent is embodied in them and where that carbon originated in the upstream supply chain. All values are in Gg CO₂-eq (gigagrams) using constant 2010 USD export weights, 2023 data.
Top 20 Bilateral Embodied Carbon Flows (2023)
Each flow band connects an exporting economy (orange nodes, left) to an importing economy (blue nodes, right). The thickness of the band is proportional to embodied carbon in Gg CO₂-eq — wider means more carbon carried in that trade corridor. This reflects total upstream emissions activated by the importing country's demand for the exporter's goods, traced through the full supply chain via the Leontief inverse. Hover over a band to see the exact value.
Carbon Source Attribution: Top 15 Exporters (2023)
Each stacked bar shows the total embodied carbon in one economy's exports, broken down by where that carbon originated in the upstream supply chain. Domestic carbon was emitted within the exporting country's own production. Other segments show how much carbon originated in specific foreign economies' production processes before being embodied in the final exported goods — capturing the GVC dimension of carbon responsibility. For example, a large China segment in Vietnam's bar reflects Chinese intermediate inputs used in Vietnamese manufacturing exports.
